### Share House or First Apartment? How to Choose Your First Home in Japan

Landing in Japan comes with a housing math problem nobody warns you about early
enough. Rent a normal apartment as a new arrival and you're facing two months'
deposit, key money (礼金), an agency fee, a guarantor you don't have, and a
completely empty room — realistically ¥400,000–750,000 before your first night's
sleep. For most people arriving with a few months' savings, that number simply
isn't payable.

So the real first decision isn't *which apartment* — it's whether to start in a
share house at all. Here's how to make that call.

#### The wall a normal apartment puts in front of you

A standard Japanese lease assumes you're already established here. You'll
typically need:

- **A guarantor (保証人)** — or a paid guarantor company, which itself charges
  50–100% of one month's rent
- **Key money (礼金)** — a non-refundable gift to the landlord, often 1–2 months'
  rent
- **A deposit (敷金)** — usually 1–2 months
- **An agency fee** — commonly one month
- **Furniture and appliances** — most apartments come with *nothing*, not even a
  ceiling light

Add it up and the upfront cost dwarfs the monthly rent. It's the single biggest
reason a first apartment goes wrong for new arrivals: not the rent, the entry
fee.

#### What a share house removes

A share house (シェアハウス) is a furnished private room with shared common spaces
— kitchen, bathroom, living area — run by a professional operator rather than an
individual landlord. For a newcomer, the appeal is what it *deletes* from the
list above:

- No guarantor
- No key money
- A small deposit (often one month, sometimes less)
- Furniture and appliances included
- Bills usually bundled into one monthly payment
- Move-in within days, and minimum stays as short as one month

You trade some privacy for a move-in cost of roughly ¥70,000–130,000 instead of
¥400,000+. For your first 3–12 months, that trade is often the smart choice, not
the compromise — and foreigner-focused operators like Sakura House and Borderless
House run English-language application processes you can start before you even
arrive. (For a full operator-by-operator and city-by-city breakdown, Expat
Japan's [guide to share houses in Japan for foreigners][sh] is a good next read.)

#### The international-student case

If you're arriving on a student visa, the share house route is even more clearly
the answer. You almost certainly don't have a Japanese guarantor or a salary to
show a landlord — the two things a normal lease demands. A share house sidesteps
both: a student visa and residence card are enough to apply, enrollment letters
or proof of funds are typically accepted, and the short minimum stays line up
with academic terms. Pick one within cycling distance of your school and you've
also solved your commute and your first friendships in one move.

#### When a normal apartment actually wins

Share houses aren't the permanent answer for everyone. Choose a private
apartment once these are true:

- You've been in Japan six months or more and have saved for move-in costs
- You value quiet and privacy over cost savings
- You're staying long-term and want a real home base
- You have a guarantor company lined up
- You're living with a partner, or you simply need your own space to function

The common pattern that works: **start in a share house, use those months to
build savings, a credit history, and a guarantor relationship, then move into
your own place from a position of strength** — instead of emptying your account
in week one.

#### The bottom line

Your first home in Japan is a cash-flow decision as much as a lifestyle one. If
you're new, under-capitalized, or on a student visa, a share house clears the
entry wall that stops most people cold. If you're settled and funded, a private
apartment gives you the space and privacy a share house can't. Match the choice
to where you actually are — not to where you'd like to be in two years.
